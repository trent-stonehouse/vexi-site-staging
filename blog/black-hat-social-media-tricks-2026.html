Black-hat watch: the social media tricks people are trying right now, and what happens to the accounts

Engagement pods, comment bots, sub4sub, bought views, AI-made “customers”, stuffed business names, gated reviews, fake listings and reupload farms. What each one is, what the rules say, and what really happens to the account.

IN SHORT

  • Most black-hat tricks fake a signal the platforms already measure: likes, comments, subscribers, views or reviews.
  • Every platform here bans them in its own policy pages. The usual result is removed engagement, less reach, strikes or a suspended account.
  • Fake reviews, fake testimonials and bought followers used for business cross into US law. Everything else has an honest version that does the same job.

Every few months a new “growth hack” goes around. Most are old ones with a new name. We read the rules for each one, on the platform's own pages, and checked what the platforms say they do about it.

This is reporting, not a how-to. You will find what people are trying, why it tempts them, and the honest risk.

How to read the labels

Each trick gets one label. Fine means it is allowed. Risky means it is allowed but often costs you reach. Against the rules means the platform bans it and can act on the account. Illegal means it also breaks the law, in the US at least.

One pattern runs through all of them. Each platform ranks on signals: watch time, likes, comments, subscribers, reviews. A black-hat trick fakes one of those signals. The platforms know this, so their rules are written around it.

Engagement pods

What it is. A group chat where everyone agrees to like and comment on each other's posts within minutes of posting. Why people do it. Early engagement looks like a hit, and a hit gets shown to more people.

The rules. Meta's spam standard bans selling, buying or exchanging engagement such as likes, shares, views and follows. A pod is an exchange. It also bans very high-frequency activity, by hand or automatic, and says accounts can be restricted even at lower rates when other spam signs show up.

What happens. Restrictions on liking, commenting or following are the common result. The quieter cost: pod likes come from people who will never buy from you. The honest version: a real collab post with a business your clients already trust. Label: Against the rules.

Comment bots and auto-replies

What it is. Tools that post the same comment, or an AI-written one, under hundreds of videos. “Great tips! Check out my page.” Why people do it. Each comment is a tiny ad, and it costs nothing.

The rules. YouTube's spam policy bans high-volume, repetitive comments that drive traffic, and its own example is the “check out my channel” message pasted across hundreds of videos. TikTok's integrity rules ban automation tools and scripts built to get around its systems. Meta bans very high-frequency activity, manual or automatic.

What happens. YouTube can remove the content and issue a warning or a strike, and three strikes in 90 days can end a channel. TikTok lists content removal and bans. The honest version: reply by hand to the comments on your own posts. AI can draft a reply. A person should send it. Label: Against the rules.

Sub4sub and paid-for likes

What it is. “Subscribe to me and I will subscribe to you.” Or a giveaway where the entry is a like and a subscribe. Why people do it. A bigger number looks like trust.

The rules. YouTube's spam policy bans offering rewards in exchange for likes, views or subscribers, and names sub-for-sub schemes as an example. It also says it is okay to ask viewers to like, comment or subscribe.

What happens. The same warning, strike and termination path as other spam. A swapped subscriber does not watch, so the channel looks big and performs small. The honest version: ask. A clear “subscribe if you want the next one” is allowed. Label: Against the rules.

Bought views and followers

What it is. Paying a site for views, likes or followers, often from bots or hijacked accounts. Why people do it. It is fast, and people trust a big number.

The rules. YouTube's fake engagement policy bans it and says channels may be terminated. TikTok says it removes fake likes and followers, can restrict posting and search visibility, and can ban the account and any new ones made to get around the ban. Meta bans buying engagement outright.

The law. The FTC's 2024 rule bans buying or selling fake indicators of social media influence, such as followers or views from bots or hijacked accounts, when the buyer knew or should have known and used them for business. It lets the FTC seek civil penalties. The honest version: fewer, better videos, and paid ads if you want reach you can pay for. Label: Illegal for a business in the US.

AI-made “customers”

What it is. A generated person in a generated kitchen, saying how much they love your product. It looks like a happy client filmed it. Why people do it. Real client videos are hard to get. AI ones take minutes.

The law. The FTC's rule bans fake reviews and testimonials from people who do not exist, and names AI-generated ones. An AI actor posing as a real customer is a fake testimonial. The rules. TikTok requires a label on AI-generated content that shows realistic scenes or people. YouTube requires you to disclose realistic altered or synthetic content, and repeated failure can bring penalties. Meta says it may require an AI label on photorealistic video and realistic audio, with penalties when one is missing.

The honest version: an AI presenter who is plainly a presenter, labelled, never posing as a client. Better still, ask a real client to film ten seconds on their phone. Label: Illegal when it poses as a real customer.

Stolen trends and reupload farms

What it is. Downloading someone else's viral video and posting it as yours. Or running a page that pumps out compilations and near-copies, often made with AI, by the hundred. Why people do it. The video already worked once.

The rules. YouTube's spam policy bans re-posting other people's videos without adding anything of your own, and bans using AI or automation to churn out lots of near-identical videos. In July 2025 YouTube renamed its monetization rule on repeated content to inauthentic content, and said such content has always been ineligible. TikTok says unoriginal content with nothing new added is not eligible for the For You feed. Facebook says accounts that keep reusing others' work lose monetization for a while and get reduced distribution on everything they share.

What happens. Lost reach, lost monetization, removed videos, and copyright claims from the owner. The honest version: join the trend, not the video. Use the format or the sound with your own footage and your own take. That is Fine. Label: Against the rules for the copy.

Google Business Profile: stuffed names, gated reviews, fake listings

Keyword-stuffed names. “Smith Plumbing” becomes “Smith Plumbing Emergency Plumber Dallas 24/7.” People try it because words in the name seem to help. Google's guidelines say unnecessary information in the name is not permitted and could get the profile suspended. Label: Against the rules.

Review gating. Sending a review link only to happy clients, and sending the rest to a private form. Google's contribution policy bans selectively asking for positive reviews and discouraging negative ones. Google removes those reviews, and serious or repeated cases can lose account privileges. The FTC rule separately bans threats to get bad reviews taken down. Label: Against the rules.

Fake listings. Extra profiles at a rented mailbox or a friend's address, to show up in more towns. Google requires a real-world location and says a virtual office is not eligible. At the far end is fraud: in March 2025 Google sued an alleged scam ring over fake and hijacked listings and said it had removed more than 10,000. Label: Against the rules, and Illegal when it fakes a business.

The honest version: your real name, your real address or a service area, and a review link sent to every client. Our Google Business Profile guide covers what does move the map.

What is folklore

Some fears are folklore. “Shadowban” is a popular word, but it is not a term on any of the rule pages above. What they describe is narrower: posts that are not eligible to be recommended. A reach drop can also have a plainer cause, like a weaker video. Instagram's Account Status screen shows whether your posts can be recommended to non-followers, and why not if they cannot. Check it before you blame a ban.

The honest tricks work, and they are steady work. Our guides for YouTube and Instagram and TikTok list them.

Questions, answered.

Are engagement pods against Instagram’s rules?

Yes. Meta’s spam standard bans selling, buying or exchanging engagement such as likes, shares, views and follows. A pod is an exchange of engagement.

Is buying followers illegal?

In the US, the FTC’s 2024 rule bans buying or selling fake indicators of social media influence, such as bot followers or views, when the buyer knew or should have known they were fake and used them for business.

Can I use AI actors in my ads?

An AI presenter who is plainly a presenter, and labelled where the platform asks, is allowed. An AI person posing as a real customer is a fake testimonial, which the FTC’s rule bans.

What is review gating?

Asking only happy clients for a review, and steering unhappy ones away. Google’s contribution policy bans it and removes the reviews it finds.

Will it post without asking me?

Only if you let it. Autopilot can wait for your approval on everything, or run on its own and post at the times that perform.
